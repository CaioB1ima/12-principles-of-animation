# Origin, historiography and context

## Who developed the principles

The **12 Basic Principles of Animation** were developed by Disney and
introduced by **Ollie Johnston** and **Frank Thomas** in their 1981 book
*_The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation_*. They based their book on the
work of Disney's leading animators of the 1930s, who sought to produce more
realistic animation.

## The original purpose

The main objective was to produce an **illusion** that animated characters
obeyed the **basic laws of physics**, but also to address more abstract
issues, such as emotional timing and character appeal.

## The "Bible of animation"

The book and some of its principles were adopted by some traditional
animation studios and have been cited by some as the "**Bible of
animation**". In 1999, the book was voted the number one "best animation
book of all time" in an online poll by _Animation World Network_.

## Why they are still relevant?

Although originally conceived for **traditional animations**, made by hand,
the principles retain great relevance for current animations, in which
**computer animation** prevails.

- **Lasseter (1987):** John Lasseter wrote
  "Principles of traditional animation applied to 3D computer animation"
  (SIGGRAPH, 1987), claiming the 12 principles for 3D. This text was
  decisive for the principles to become the common foundation of modern
  animation.
- **Digital:** computer animation removes part of the difficulties of
  "straight-ahead" (proportion), but "pose-to-pose" is still used because
  of its compositional advantages. Computers make the method easier and
  can automatically fill in missing intermediate sequences.

## Disney and the "Nine Old Men"

The animators who shaped the Disney style of the 1930s became known as the
**"Nine Old Men"**. Johnston and Thomas, who were part of this group and
were taught by the elders, gathered and formalized the techniques in
*The Illusion of Life*.
