# How to apply the principles in animation

The 12 principles were made for hand-drawn animation, but today they serve
as fundamental guidelines in any tool. Below are practical suggestions for
application by medium.

## Traditional animation (2D hand-drawn)

1. **Squash and Stretch:** observe a person bending to jump; the body
   elongates when rising and squashes when falling.
2. **Anticipation:** use the "counter-move" before any punch or jump.
3. **Staging:** ensure the viewer's gaze falls on the important action,
   using light, placement, and camera.
4. **Straight Ahead / Pose to Pose:** combine — fluid action graphics with
   "straight-ahead", emotional scenes with "pose-to-pose". Use the
   thumbnail to test composition.
5. **Follow-through and overlapping:** slack in arms, hair, and clothing.
6. **Slow-in / Slow-out:** press harder on extreme frames; too rigid makes
   "jumps".
7. **Arcs:** the body speaks in arcs; the movement of arm and leg should be
   curved, never straight, except for mechanical movement.
8. **Secondary action:** a hand on hip, hair swaying, a falling pillow —
   all reinforce the primary action without stealing the focus.
9. **Timing:** the number of frames per action defines weight, mood, and
   character.
10. **Exaggeration:** exaggerate toward style; in "dramatic" animation
    the exaggeration is subtle, in caricature it is explicit.
11. **Solid drawing:** volume, weight, balance, light; avoid "twins"
    (identical left-right mirroring).
12. **Appeal:** beauty, symmetry, proportion — although a villain can
    also have appeal.

## 2D/3D computer animation

- The principle of **Straight Ahead** becomes less limiting: keyframing
  allows precise control of poses, and "pose-to-pose" is the standard.
- **Follow-through** and **overlapping** appear naturally in rigging and
  body deformers (IK/FK, skin).
- **Squash and Stretch** and **Arcs** are critical in 3D, where camera
  animation and keyframe spacing indicate weight.
- **Solid drawing** in 3D means keeping camera, lighting, and modeling
  consistent; "drawing" is done in 3D modeling today, not on paper.
- **Appeal** in 3D depends on silhouette, proportions, and character design.

## Character, object, and scene animation

- **Character:** action + facial expression + appeal. The character is the
  center.
- **Object:** weight, elasticity, inertia. A tennis ball and a steel ball
  have the same trajectory, but different timing and squash-and-stretch.
- **Scene:** staging, composition, contrast, rhythm, and framing.

## Quick checklist

Before finishing an animation, ask yourself:
- [ ] Does the movement have a beginning (before) and an end (after) —
  anticipation?
- [ ] Is the weight credible in the extreme moments — slow-in/out?
- [ ] Does the movement follow arcs, not straight lines — arcs?
- [ ] Is there something else moving (pendulum, shadow, clothing) — overlap?
- [ ] Does the primary action not get lost in unnecessary detail — secondary?
- [ ] Is the character visually interesting — appeal?
- [ ] Are there no "twins" (dead symmetry) — solid drawing?
